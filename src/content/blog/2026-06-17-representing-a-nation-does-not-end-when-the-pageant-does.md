There are things meant to last only for a season: crowns, sashes, titles. But pride in one’s country should never be one of them.

That is why the recent controversy involving pageant personalities Brandon Espiritu and Jether Palomo struck a nerve far beyond the pageant community. Their remarks–including references to “halfies” and comments that many interpreted as diminishing Philippine-born aspirants–sparked backlash among Filipinos who had once celebrated them as representatives of the nation. 

Whether their statements were poorly worded or taken out of context, they touched something deeper than a social media controversy—they touched identity. Because the issue was never about mixed heritage. It was about humility.

No one questions the achievements of Filipinos with diverse backgrounds. In fact, the Philippines has long embraced them. Time and time again, Filipinos have opened their arms to those who choose to carry the country’s name, regardless of bloodline, accent, or birthplace. The Filipino identity has never been measured in percentages.

Not half. Not quarter. Not fractions. But belonging.

And belonging demands respect.

What disappointed many Filipinos was not the existence of “halfies”. It was the implication–intentional or not–that excellence somehow belongs more naturally to some Filipinos than to others. As though foreign blood makes one more deserving of admiration. As though a nation of more than a hundred million people needs validation from somewhere else before it can shine.

It does not.

The Philippines has never lacked beauty.

It has never lacked talent. And it certainly has never lacked pride.

Perhaps what stung the most was the growing perception that some people are eager to wear the country’s sash when it offers opportunities, titles, and international exposure, but become distant from the very people who embrace them when the applause fades.

Representation should never feel transactional. A nation is not a stepping stone, patriotism is not a convenience, and identity is not something to be claimed only when it is rewarding. Because those who benefit from the country’s love, support, and recognition have a responsibility to honor the people who made those opportunities possible.

To represent the Philippines is not to stand above it. It is to stand for it, for the flag pinned on one’s chest is not a prop. It is a trust bestowed by millions whose stories, struggles, and aspirations cannot be reduced to labels. And that trust demands gratitude.

Crowns fade. Titles change hands. Applause dies down. But the country remains.

Long after the cameras stop rolling and the spotlights dim, what endures is not the title one held, but the respect one showed to the nation that gave that title meaning.

National identity and representation are responsibilities, not accessories. Pride in one’s country should not disappear when the spotlight does. Because a sash may come off. A crown may be passed on. But if love for one’s country disappears when the pageant ends, then perhaps what was worn all along was never patriotism—only fabric.
