A team-building activity is supposed to strengthen trust. Yet on June 8, two young athletes who carried the hopes of their families, their teammates, and their future careers never returned home. Rene Baterbonia and Divine Adili drowned during an Ateneo basketball team-building activity in Dipaculao, Aurora. 

The tragedy has since triggered investigations by the Department of Justice (DOJ), the National Bureau of Investigation (NBI), and the Criminal Investigation and Detection Group (CIDG). What unsettles me most is not merely that two student-athletes died. It is the possibility that the very adults entrusted with protecting them have failed in that duty. 

We often describe safety as a priority. The language sounds reassuring. Schools print it in manuals. Coaches repeat it in orientations. Administrators invoke it after every crisis. Yet priorities are negotiable. They shift when schedules become crowded, when objectives become ambitious, or when leaders become overconfident. Responsibilities are different. Responsibilities are non-negotiable. When parents entrust schools and coaches with their children, the obligation to protect life is not optional. It is not conditional, it is the first duty from which every other duty flows.

The strongest evidence that this tragedy demands accountability lies in a simple question: Why were student-athletes exposed to a foreseeable risk in the first place? According to reports from local authorities and statements cited in media coverage of the incident, the activity took place hundreds of meters away from the resort’s main beachfront area, and resort personnel had allegedly advised the group to remain closer to shore because of unstable sea conditions. If those accounts are ultimately confirmed, then the issue ceases to be bad luck. It becomes a matter of judgment, and judgement is precisely what leadership is supposed to provide. 

Defenders of institutional silence often argue that accidents happen. Of course they do. Storms form. Currents shift. Human beings make mistakes. But modern safety principles are built on a recognition that many disasters occur not because danger was invisible, but because warnings were overlooked. In aviation, medicine, engineering, and sports management, the central question after a tragedy is rarely "What went wrong?" Instead, it is "What safeguards failed?"

When two young men drown during an organized activity, society has every right to examine whether adequate precautions, supervision, risk assessments, and emergency protocols existed before the first participant entered the water.

The consequences extend far beyond one university or one basketball program. Across the country, schools promote camps, retreats, leadership seminars, athletic training sessions, and team-building activities as opportunities for growth. Most are conducted responsibly. Yet this case exposes a troubling culture that sometimes romanticizes hardship as character formation. Our society often celebrates toughness and endurance, rewarding those who push beyond their limits. But when challenge is mistaken for safety and risk is confused with resilience, the line between growth and recklessness begins to disappear.

Somewhere along the way, some leaders begin to mistake risk for resilience. The result is a dangerous belief that the more uncomfortable an activity becomes, the more valuable it must be. History repeatedly proves otherwise. Growth requires challenge–it does not require recklessness.

What troubles me even more is the unequal power dynamic at the center of this tragedy. Student-athletes are conditioned to obey authority. They trust coaches because coaches control opportunities, playing time, scholarships, and development. That trust creates a moral burden on every adult leader. The greater the authority, the greater the responsibility. When students follow instructions, they should never have to wonder whether those instructions will place their lives in jeopardy. The burden of caution always belongs to the adult.

 There is also a quieter tragedy hidden beneath the headlines. Public discussion has understandably focused on investigations, legal liability, and institutional responses. Yet at the heart of this story are two families whose futures were permanently altered. One lost a son preparing for a promising collegiate career. Another lost a young man who crossed continents in pursuit of opportunity through sport. Their dreams did not drown because of a lack of talent or determination. They were interrupted by circumstances that remain under investigation and that demand answers. 

To be fair, some caution against assigning blame before investigations conclude. They are right about one thing: due process matters. Facts matter. Responsible journalism requires restraint. No individual should be convicted in the court of public opinion before evidence is fully examined. However, demanding accountability is not the same as prejudging guilt. Accountability begins with transparency. It begins with honest answers to difficult questions. It begins with leaders showing up, cooperating fully, and demonstrating that protecting students matters more than protecting reputations.

Recent developments only reinforce the seriousness of the case. The DOJ ordered an NBI task force investigation. The CIDG has publicly stated that it is examining possible violations that may include homicide or anti-hazing provisions, depending on the evidence gathered. Local authorities have likewise scrutinized the circumstances surrounding the activity. These actions do not prove wrongdoing. They do prove that the questions are significant enough to merit the highest levels of investigation.

The irony is impossible to ignore. A team-building activity was meant to strengthen bonds and cultivate trust, yet it ended in a tragedy that exposed the consequences of neglected responsibility. When safety becomes secondary to objectives, institutions risk becoming exactly what they never intended to be: built to break.

Team-building is meant to strengthen bonds, develop trust, and create unity. Yet when safety is treated as secondary, the very process designed to build people can break them instead. Institutions often measure success through trophies, championships, and prestige. I believe a more important measure exists. The true test of any school, coach, or organization is not how many victories it produces. It is how faithfully it protects the people entrusted to its care.

Rene Baterbonia and Divine Adili deserved futures, not investigations. They deserved mentorship, not uncertainty. They deserved protection, not preventable danger. If their deaths leave the nation with any obligation, it is this: never allow the language of excellence to excuse the abandonment of responsibility. Achievement built on neglected safety is not excellence at all. It is failure wearing the uniform of success.
